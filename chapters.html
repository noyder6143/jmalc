🪟 Chapter 1: Operating Systems

The operating system (OS) is the boss program on every computer, tablet, and phone. It runs the hardware, starts your apps, and gives you a screen to work on. This chapter is about Windows 10 first, plus what is the same on Macs, Linux, and phones.

What an operating system does

  • Controls the hardware: memory, storage, screen, keyboard, printer, network.
  • Runs apps and keeps them from crashing into each other.
  • Gives you the interface: the desktop, windows, icons, and menus (a graphical user interface, or GUI). Very old systems used typed commands only.
  • Keeps each person's files and settings separate with user accounts.

Popular operating systems

OSMade byWhere you see itOpen or closed?
Windows (10, 11)MicrosoftMost PCs and laptopsProprietary (closed)
macOSAppleMac computers onlyProprietary
Linux (Ubuntu and others)CommunityServers, some desktopsOpen source
UNIXSeveral companiesBig servers; macOS grew from itMostly proprietary
ChromeOSGoogleChromebooksBuilt on Linux
iOS / iPadOSAppleiPhone, iPadProprietary
AndroidGoogleMost other phones and tabletsOpen source base
  • Proprietary: one company owns the code. You buy a license and agree to the EULA (end user license agreement).
  • Open source: the code is public. Anyone can study it, change it, and share it, often for free.
  • Versions are releases over time (Windows 10, Windows 11). Editions are packages of one version for different buyers (Home, Pro, Enterprise).

User accounts and profiles

Account typeWhat it can do
AdministratorChange settings for everyone, install or remove programs and hardware, create and manage other accounts
Standard userUse programs and change only your own settings. Asks an admin to approve big changes
Guest / child accountsLimited use, often with family safety rules
  • A Microsoft account (email sign-in) syncs your settings across PCs. A local account lives on one PC only.
  • Your user profile holds your desktop, documents folder, wallpaper, and app settings. Each person on the PC gets their own profile.
  • Sign in with a password, PIN, picture password, or fingerprint/face (Windows Hello).

Turning it on and off

OptionWhat happensUse it when
Shut downCloses everything and powers offDone for the day, moving the computer, or adding hardware
RestartShuts down, then starts againAfter updates or installs, or when things act strange
SleepKeeps your work in RAM, uses a little power, wakes fastShort breaks
HibernateSaves your work to the drive, then powers offLong breaks on a laptop, to save battery
LockHides your screen; your apps keep runningStepping away for a minute
Sign outCloses your apps and leaves the PC on for someone elseShared or public computers
Switch userLeaves you signed in while someone else signs inShared family PC
  • Always shut down from the Start menu Power button. Holding the power button is only for a frozen computer, because you can lose unsaved work.

Getting around the Windows desktop

PartWhat it is for
Start button and Start menuOpen apps, search, settings, and the Power button
Search box (Cortana in Windows 10)Type to find apps, files, and settings

Action Center (Windows 10)Notifications and quick settings like Wi-Fi and Airplane mode

  • Mouse: click to select, double-click to open, right-click for a shortcut menu, drag to move.
  • Keyboard: Windows key opens Start; Ctrl+C copy, Ctrl+V paste, Ctrl+Z undo; Alt+Tab switches windows.
  • Touch: tap = click, press and hold = right-click, swipe to scroll, pinch or spread to zoom.

Global settings vs. profile settings

Global (whole computer, every user)Profile-specific (just you)
Screen resolutionDesktop background (wallpaper)
Date and timeTheme colors and lock screen picture
Power and sleep plansStart menu and taskbar pins
Installing hardware and programsScreen saver, mouse and sound preferences
  • Settings app: the modern place for most options (Start > gear icon).
  • Control Panel: the older, classic tool. Still has some options the Settings app doesn't.
  • Global changes usually need an administrator.

Mobile operating systems

  • Power button: press to wake or lock; press and hold to power off or restart.
  • Lock screen: shows time and alerts; swipe and enter your PIN, pattern, fingerprint, or face to unlock.
  • Home screen: app icons and widgets. Swipe between pages.
  • Settings app: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, notifications, display, battery, updates.
  • Virtual assistants answer voice questions: Siri (Apple), Google Assistant (Android), Cortana (Windows 10).

Operating system updates

Windows update typeWhat it contains
ImportantSecurity and critical fixes. Install right away
RecommendedBug fixes and improved features
OptionalExtra drivers or trial software you pick yourself
  • Windows 10 and 11 download and install updates automatically. You can pause them for a while or set active hours so the PC won't restart while you work.
  • Check by hand: Settings > Update & Security > Windows Update > Check for updates (Windows 11: Settings > Windows Update).
  • Good: you get security fixes fast. Bad: a surprise restart or, rarely, an update that breaks a driver.
  • Phones: Settings > Software update (Android) or Settings > General > Software Update (iPhone). Plug in and use Wi-Fi first.

✅ Know for the test

  • Administrator vs. standard user: only admins install programs and change settings for everyone.
  • Sleep keeps work in RAM; hibernate saves it to the drive and turns off. Lock keeps apps running; sign out closes them.
  • Wallpaper and theme are profile settings. Resolution, date and time, and power plans are global.
  • Right-click (or press and hold on touch) opens a shortcut menu.
  • Important updates are security fixes. Windows 10/11 installs updates automatically.
  • Windows and macOS are proprietary. Linux is open source. Android is built on open source.

🧠 Quick self-check

Answer in your head, then tap a question to see the answer.

Q1. You will be away from your laptop for a weekend and want your open files waiting when you come back. Which power option?

Answer: Hibernate. It saves your work to the drive and powers off, so the battery isn't drained.

Q2. Which account type can install a new program for everyone on the PC?

Answer: An administrator account.

Q3. You change the desktop background. Does your sister's account on the same PC change too?

Answer: No. The background is a profile-specific setting.

Q4. What does pressing the Windows key + Tab open?

Answer: Task View, where you see open windows and can make a new virtual desktop.

Q5. Name the three kinds of Windows updates.

Answer: Important, recommended, and optional.

🖥️ Chapter 2: Hardware

Hardware is the physical stuff you can touch. This chapter explains how hardware is measured, the difference between memory and storage, the main kinds of computers, input devices, power plans, ports, and wireless connections.

Drivers, firmware, and platforms

TermPlain meaningExample
Device driverSoftware inside the OS that lets it talk to one piece of hardwarePrinter driver, graphics driver
FirmwareA small program stored on the device's own chip that runs the hardware at the most basic levelBIOS/UEFI on a PC, phone or router firmware
PlatformThe hardware plus OS combination that apps are built forWindows PC, Mac, Android phone, iPhone
  • Update drivers after a big OS upgrade or if a device stops working right. Windows Update handles most of them.
  • An app built for one platform usually won't run on another (an iPhone app won't install on Windows).

Units of measure

UnitShortAbout how much
BitbOne 0 or 1, the smallest piece of data
ByteB8 bits, about one typed character
KilobyteKBAbout 1,000 bytes (1,024). A short text file
MegabyteMBAbout 1,000 KB. A photo or a song
GigabyteGBAbout 1,000 MB. A movie; phone and RAM sizes
TerabyteTBAbout 1,000 GB. A big hard drive
Hertz / GHzHzCycles per second. CPU speed is in gigahertz (billions per second)
Bits per secondKbps, Mbps, GbpsNetwork and internet speed (bandwidth)
  • Capital B = bytes (storage). Small b = bits (speed). 100 Mbps is megabits per second, not megabytes.

Memory vs. storage

Memory (RAM)Storage (drive)
JobShort-term workspace for open appsLong-term home for files and programs
When power is offErased (volatile)Kept (non-volatile)
Typical size4 to 32 GB128 GB to several TB
Too little causesSlow, freezing, crashes with many apps openCan't save or install; device out of space
FixClose apps and tabs, restart, add RAMDelete or move files, empty the Recycle Bin, add a drive or use the cloud
  • Storage devices: hard disk drive (HDD, spinning, cheap and large), solid state drive (SSD, fast, no moving parts), USB flash drive, memory card, external drive, CD/DVD/Blu-ray.
  • Local storage is inside or plugged into your device. Remote storage is on another computer: a network drive or the cloud.

Types of computers

TypeBest forTrade-offs
ServerSends files, websites, email, or apps to other computers on a networkPowerful and runs all the time; usually managed by IT
DesktopPower, big screens, easy to upgradeNot portable
LaptopWork anywhere with a built-in screen, keyboard, and batteryHarder to upgrade; battery limits
ChromebookLow cost, web apps, cloud storage (ChromeOS)Small local storage; needs internet for most things
TabletTouch, reading, video, light workTyping and big programs are harder
SmartphoneCalls, texts, apps, camera, GPSSmall screen; battery and data limits

Keyboards, mice, and touch

  • Keyboard keys: letters and numbers, function keys (F1 to F12; F1 is often Help), arrow keys, Ctrl, Alt, Shift, and the Windows key.
  • Keyboards connect by USB cable or wirelessly (Bluetooth or a small USB receiver).
  • Pointing devices: mouse, touchpad on laptops, and stylus (a pen for touch screens).
  • Touch screens are easy to learn and need no extra parts, but they are less precise than a mouse and smudge easily.
  • Smartphone hardware: touch screen, cameras, microphone, speaker, GPS, sensors, battery, and a SIM card that identifies you to the cell carrier.

Power plans and battery life

Windows planWhat it does
BalancedFull speed when needed, saves energy when idle (default)
Power saverDims the screen and slows down to stretch battery
High performanceMaximum speed, uses the most power
  • Find it: Control Panel > Power Options, or Settings > System > Power & sleep (Windows 11: Power & battery).
  • Battery killers: bright screen, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth left on, many apps running, video and games, an old battery.

Ports and peripherals

PortUsed for
USB (Type-A, Type-C)Almost everything: mouse, keyboard, printer, flash drive, phone charging
HDMIDigital video and sound to a TV or monitor

Ethernet (RJ-45)Wired network cable
Audio jacks (3.5 mm)Headphones, speakers, microphone
PS/2, serial, parallelOld ports for keyboards, mice, and printers
  • Peripherals are devices you add on: monitor, printer, scanner, speakers, webcam, external drive, digital camera.
  • A second monitor can mirror your screen (duplicate) or give you more space (extend). Press Windows key + P to choose.

Wireless connections

TechnologyRangeTypical use
Wi-FiA home or buildingInternet and network access
BluetoothAbout 30 feet (10 m)Earbuds, keyboards, mice, car audio. Devices must be paired
Infrared (IR)Short; needs a clear line of sightTV remotes, some older devices
CellularMiles, from towersPhones and cellular tablets

✅ Know for the test

  • RAM is temporary and volatile; storage is permanent. More RAM helps multitasking; more storage holds more files.
  • Order: bit, byte, KB, MB, GB, TB. Storage uses bytes; speed uses bits per second.
  • A server answers requests from other computers.
  • A driver lives in the OS; firmware lives on the device.
  • HDMI carries video and sound; VGA is old analog video; RJ-45 is Ethernet.
  • Bluetooth is short range and must be paired; infrared needs line of sight.
  • Power saver stretches battery; high performance drains it.

🧠 Quick self-check

Answer in your head, then tap a question to see the answer.

Q1. Your laptop freezes when you have 30 browser tabs open. What hardware upgrade helps most?

Answer: More RAM (memory).

Q2. Which is bigger, 500 MB or 2 GB?

Answer: 2 GB. One GB is about 1,000 MB.

Q3. Which port sends both video and sound to a TV?

Answer: HDMI.

Q4. Wireless earbuds need to be connected to your phone the first time. What is that called?

Answer: Pairing (Bluetooth).

Q5. What is the difference between a driver and firmware?

Answer: A driver is software in the OS that talks to a device. Firmware is built into the device's own chip.

📶 Chapter 3: Networks and Mobile Devices

A network is two or more devices linked so they can share data, printers, and an internet connection. This chapter covers how home and business networks work, how you get internet service, wired and wireless connections, cell service, landlines, and voice mail.

Network basics

  • Networks let people share files, printers, and one internet connection, and let you stream (watch while it arrives) or download (save a full copy first).
  • TCP/IP is the set of rules (protocols) every device on the internet uses to send data.
  • LAN (local area network): one home, office, or school. WAN (wide area network): links places far apart. The internet is the biggest WAN.
  • Businesses also use intranets (private, for employees) and extranets (shared with partners).

IP addresses

IdeaWhat to know
IP addressThe number that identifies each device on a network
IPv4Four numbers 0 to 255 with dots, like 192.168.1.10
IPv6Newer and much longer, with letters and colons; made because IPv4 addresses ran out
Private addressUsed inside your home or office LAN (often starts 192.168 or 10.)
Public addressGiven to your router by your ISP; what websites see
Find yoursSettings > Network details, or type ipconfig in Command Prompt
  • Your router hands out private addresses to each device automatically (DHCP). Two devices on one network can't share the same address.

Getting internet service

ServiceComes overNotes
Dial-upPhone line, by calling inVery slow and mostly gone
DSLPhone company's copper linesSpeed depends on distance from the phone office
CableTV company's coaxial cableFast, but shared with neighbors, so it slows at busy times
Fiber (like FiOS)Glass strands carrying lightFastest; not available everywhere
SatelliteDish pointed at a satelliteWorks in rural areas; slower response and data limits
Cellular (4G, 5G) and hotspotsCell towersWorks on the go; usage counts against your data plan
  • Broadband means always-on, high-speed internet (everything here except dial-up).
  • An ISP (internet service provider) sells you the connection. Ads say speeds are "up to" a number because real speed is usually lower.
  • Download speed (data coming to you) is usually much faster than upload speed (data you send).
  • Run a speed test website to check what you really get.

Home network hardware

DeviceJob
ModemTranslates the ISP's signal so your network can use it
RouterShares one internet connection with all your devices and directs traffic between networks
SwitchAdds more wired Ethernet ports to a LAN
Wireless access pointLets Wi-Fi devices join the network (most home routers have one built in)
Network interface card (NIC)The part in each device that connects it to a network, wired or wireless

Wired (Ethernet) vs. wireless (Wi-Fi)

Wired EthernetWi-Fi
Speed and reliabilityFaster and steadierCan slow down with distance, walls, and interference
SecurityHarder to break into; someone needs a cableMust be protected with a password and encryption
ConvenienceStuck where the cable reachesMove around freely; works for phones and tablets
ConnectorRJ-45 cable (Cat5e, Cat6)Radio waves (2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands)
  • Wi-Fi standards are named 802.11 (a, b, g, n, ac, and newer). Newer ones are faster.
  • 2.4 GHz reaches farther but is slower and more crowded. 5 GHz is faster but shorter range.
  • Secure Wi-Fi uses WPA2 or WPA3 encryption and a strong password. WEP is old and weak. Change the router's default admin password.
  • WPS lets you connect by pressing a button on the router, but it is less secure.
  • Join Wi-Fi: click the network icon, pick the network name, enter the password, Connect.
  • Shared or network printers can be added from Settings > Devices > Printers & scanners.

Cellular networks and service

  • Phones talk by radio to cell towers. Each tower covers an area (a cell) and hands calls off as you move.
  • Carriers (Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile and others) own the networks. Coverage maps show where service is strong.
  • Cell service is separate from your home internet; the carrier, not an ISP, bills you for calls, texts, and data.
  • Contract plans: lower phone price, but an early termination fee if you leave. Prepaid plans: pay first, no contract, no credit check.
  • Plans bundle minutes, texts, and data. Add-ons include hotspot use, international calling, and insurance.
  • Roaming means using another carrier's network outside your area, which can cost extra.

Cellular devices

DeviceCan doLimits
SmartphoneCalls, texts, apps, internet, camera, GPSBattery, screen size, data limits
Basic cell phoneCalls and textsFew or no apps
Cellular tabletInternet anywhere with a data planMay not make regular phone calls; needs its own plan
Wi-Fi-only tabletInternet where Wi-Fi existsNo connection away from Wi-Fi
  • Use Wi-Fi when you can to save mobile data. Turn off mobile data in Settings to avoid charges.

Landlines and business phones

Landline good pointsLandline weak points
Very reliable with clear soundStuck in one place
A corded phone works during a power outage (the line powers it)Monthly cost even if you rarely use it
911 knows your addressNo texting, apps, or internet
  • VoIP phones send calls over the internet. They stop working if the power or internet goes out.
  • Businesses use a PBX, a private phone system with extensions, transfers, and often dialing 9 for an outside line.

Voice mail

  • Set it up the first time you call your voice mail: create a PIN, record your name, record a greeting.
  • A professional greeting: your name, "please leave your name, number, and a short message", and when you'll call back.
  • Check messages often. A full mailbox means callers can't leave a message, so delete old ones.
  • Leaving a message: say your name and number slowly, the reason for the call, and repeat the number at the end.
  • Many phones show visual voice mail, a list you can tap and play in any order.

✅ Know for the test

  • Modem connects to the ISP; router shares the connection and links networks.
  • Wired is faster, steadier, and more secure; wireless is more convenient.
  • Cable is shared with neighbors; DSL uses phone lines; satellite is for rural areas.
  • Mbps measures speed. Download is usually faster than upload.
  • IPv4 has 4 numbers with dots. Every device on a network needs a unique IP address.
  • A corded landline works in a power outage; VoIP does not.
  • Professional voice mail greeting: name, request for caller's name and number, call-back promise.

🧠 Quick self-check

Answer in your head, then tap a question to see the answer.

Q1. Which device shares one internet connection with every device in your home?

Answer: The router.

Q2. Your neighbor's internet slows down every evening when everyone gets home. Which service do they probably have?

Answer: Cable, because it is shared.

Q3. Is 192.168.1.25 an IPv4 or IPv6 address?

Answer: IPv4 (four numbers separated by dots).

Q4. The power goes out. Which phone still works: a corded landline or a VoIP phone?

Answer: The corded landline.

Q5. What should a professional voice mail greeting include?

Answer: Your name, a request for the caller's name, number, and message, and that you will call back.

📁 Chapter 4: File Management

File management means keeping your work organized so you can find it, share it, and not lose it. This chapter covers drives, folders, paths, permissions, File Explorer, file types, copying and moving, searching, the Recycle Bin, and sharing files.

Drives, folders, and paths

ItemWhat it is
C: driveThe main internal drive with Windows and your programs
D:, E:, and upOther drives: second hard drive, DVD, USB flash drive, memory card
Mapped network driveA shared folder on another computer that shows up with its own letter
Folder and subfolderContainers for files; a folder inside a folder is a subfolder
PathThe full address of a file, like C:\Users\Ana\Documents\Resume.docx
  • The folder structure is often called a directory tree. Paths use backslashes (\) in Windows and forward slashes (/) on the web and on Macs.
  • When you connect a smartphone, tablet, or camera by USB, it appears in File Explorer under This PC so you can copy files from it.

Permissions

PermissionLets a user
ReadOpen and view a file but not change it
Write / ModifyChange and save the file
Full controlDo everything, including change permissions and delete
  • Permissions protect files on shared PCs and networks. An administrator sets them.
  • Local files are on your device; remote files are on a network or the cloud and may have different rules.

Using File Explorer

  • Open it with the folder icon on the taskbar or Windows key + E.
  • Navigation pane (left): Quick Access, OneDrive, This PC, Network. Address bar (top): shows the path. Ribbon or toolbar: commands.
  • Views: icons, list, details (shows date, type, size), tiles. Sort by clicking a column heading like Date modified.
  • Window controls: minimize (hide to taskbar), maximize (full screen), restore, close. Drag the title bar to move; drag an edge or corner to resize. Use scroll bars to see more.

File types and extensions

KindCommon extensions
Documents.docx (Word), .xlsx (Excel), .pptx (PowerPoint), .pdf, .txt, .rtf
Pictures.jpg / .jpeg, .png, .gif, .bmp, .tif
Audio.mp3, .wav, .m4a, .wma
Video.mp4, .mov, .avi, .wmv
Programs (executable).exe, .msi (be careful opening these)
Compressed (archive).zip, .rar, .7z
  • The extension tells Windows which app opens the file. Windows hides extensions by default; turn them on in File Explorer: View > File name extensions.
  • Don't change an extension when renaming. The file may stop opening.

Working with files and folders

TaskHow
New folderRight-click empty space > New > Folder, or Ctrl+Shift+N
RenameSelect it and press F2, or right-click > Rename
CopyCtrl+C then Ctrl+V. The original stays
MoveCtrl+X then Ctrl+V, or drag within the same drive
Select severalCtrl+click picks one at a time; Shift+click picks a range; Ctrl+A picks all
ShortcutRight-click > Send to > Desktop (create shortcut). Deleting a shortcut doesn't delete the file
  • Dragging to a folder on the same drive moves the file. Dragging to a different drive (like a USB stick) copies it.
  • Folder Options lets you show hidden files, open items with a single click, and more.

Searching and the Recycle Bin

  • Search from the taskbar box for apps, files, and settings, or use the search box in File Explorer to look inside the current folder.
  • Narrow results with filters such as date modified, kind (type), and size, or search for an extension like .pdf.
  • Deleted files from your internal drive go to the Recycle Bin. Open it and choose Restore to put a file back where it was.
  • Emptying the Recycle Bin removes the files for good. Files deleted from USB drives and network folders skip the Recycle Bin.
  • Shift+Delete deletes permanently without using the Recycle Bin.

Default locations

ItemUsually saved to
Downloads from the webDownloads folder
DocumentsDocuments folder (or OneDrive)
Photos and scansPictures folder
Music and videosMusic and Videos folders
  • Phone photos can be copied by USB cable, uploaded to the cloud (Google Photos, iCloud, OneDrive), or sent with Bluetooth or a memory card.
  • Media apps (Photos, media players) sort pictures, music, and video into albums and libraries.

Sharing files

MethodGood forWatch out for
USB flash drive or external driveBig files, no internet neededEasy to lose; can carry malware
CD / DVDOlder archivesSmall capacity, slow, few new PCs have drives
Public or shared folderPeople on the same PC or networkSet permissions carefully
Network shareOffice teamsNeeds network access and permissions
Email attachmentSmall filesSize limits around 20 to 25 MB; some file types blocked
Cloud linkLarge files and teamworkNeeds internet; choose view or edit access
  • Zipping (compressing) bundles many files into one smaller .zip file. Right-click > Send to > Compressed (zipped) folder. To use the files, right-click > Extract All.
  • Zip before emailing a group of files or when a file is a little too large. Photos and videos don't shrink much because they are already compressed.

✅ Know for the test

  • Copy keeps the original; move doesn't. Same-drive drag moves; different-drive drag copies.
  • Ctrl+click for separate files, Shift+click for a range.
  • Recycle Bin restores files from the internal drive only; emptying it is permanent.
  • Know common extensions: .docx, .xlsx, .pptx, .pdf, .jpg, .png, .mp3, .mp4, .exe, .zip.
  • Web downloads go to the Downloads folder.
  • Read permission = view only; write = change; full control = everything.
  • Zip to send many files as one; extract to use them.

🧠 Quick self-check

Answer in your head, then tap a question to see the answer.

Q1. Write the path for Notes.txt in a folder named Class inside Documents on the C: drive.

Answer: C:\Users\(your name)\Documents\Class\Notes.txt, or simply Documents\Class\Notes.txt.

Q2. You delete a file from a USB flash drive. Can you get it back from the Recycle Bin?

Answer: Usually no. Files deleted from flash drives are removed right away.

Q3. Which extension is a compressed folder?

Answer: .zip

Q4. Where does a file you download from a website usually go?

Answer: The Downloads folder.

Q5. How do you select files 1, 4, and 7 in a list, but not the ones in between?

Answer: Hold Ctrl and click each one.

💿 Chapter 5: Software

Software is the programs that let you do things on a device: write, calculate, browse, chat, and more. This chapter covers where to get software, checking requirements, installing, removing, repairing, updating, setting preferences, app stores, and messaging apps.

Getting software

☁️ Chapter 6: Cloud Computing

The cloud means using storage and apps that live on a company's servers, reached over the internet, instead of only on your own device. This chapter covers what the cloud is, cloud accounts, Google Drive, OneDrive, iCloud, Dropbox, other cloud apps, and phone notifications.

Cloud basics

BenefitsDrawbacks
Reach your files from any device, anywhereNeeds an internet connection
Easy sharing and working together at the same timeFree space is limited; more costs money
Automatic backup if your device breaks or is lostYou trust the company to keep files safe and private
Apps run in a browser, no install neededSlow internet means slow work
  • To use the cloud you need: an internet connection, a device with a browser or app, and an account with the provider.
  • Hosted services: the provider owns and runs the servers; you just sign in.

Cloud accounts

Microsoft account (Outlook.com, school or work)OneDrive, Word, Excel, PowerPoint online

  • Manage your account: change your password, turn on 2-step verification, see how much storage you've used, and sign out of devices you don't use.

Working with cloud storage

ActionWhat it means

OrganizeMake folders, rename, move, star, and delete in the web page or app
  • Deleting a synced file on one device deletes it everywhere. Most services keep it in their own recycle bin for a while.
  • OneDrive in File Explorer: a cloud icon means online-only (needs internet); a green check means it's on your device. Choose Always keep on this device for offline use.
  • Share settings: Can view (read only) or Can edit. You can share with specific people or anyone with the link. Give the least access needed.

  • Web apps (Word, Excel, PowerPoint online; Google Docs, Sheets, Slides) open and edit files right in the browser, and several people can edit at once.
  • Each service has a mobile app so you can see, upload, and share files from your phone.

Mobile notifications

  • Notifications are alerts from apps: banners, sounds, badges (number on the icon), and lock-screen messages.
  • Change them in the app's own settings, or for every app in the phone's Settings > Notifications.
  • Turn off alerts you don't need to save battery and stay focused. Use Do Not Disturb (Focus on iPhone) for quiet time, with exceptions for important people.
  • Calendar reminders and alarms create notifications on purpose.

🔒 Chapter 7: Security and Maintenance

Security keeps your accounts, data, and devices safe. Maintenance keeps them working. This chapter covers passwords, malware, network risks, public computers, scams, antivirus, firewalls, safe shopping, VPNs, backups, restore and reset options, and basic troubleshooting.

Usernames and passwords

  • Strong password: long (12+ characters), with uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols, or a passphrase of several random words.
  • Avoid names, birthdays, pet names, and common words. Use a different password for each account.
  • Never share it. Store passwords in a password manager, not on a sticky note or in an unprotected file.
  • Change a password right away if you think someone knows it. Turn on 2-step verification when offered.
  • Lock your computer when you walk away (Windows key + L). Sign out on shared computers.

Malware and other threats

ThreatWhat it does
VirusAttaches to a file or program and spreads when that file is opened or shared
WormCopies itself across networks by itself, with no file needed
TrojanPretends to be useful software but opens a back door for attackers
SpywareSecretly watches what you do and collects your information
KeyloggerRecords every key you type, including passwords
AdwareShows unwanted pop-up ads
RansomwareLocks your files and demands payment
  • Signs of infection: the computer suddenly runs slowly, pop-ups appear, programs crash, settings change, or your friends get strange messages from you.

Network risks

ConnectionRiskProtection
Wired (Ethernet)Lower; someone needs physical accessLock up network equipment
Home Wi-FiNeighbors or strangers can join or snoopWPA2 or WPA3, strong Wi-Fi password, change router admin password
Public Wi-Fi (cafe, airport)Others on the network may see your traffic; fake hotspotsAvoid banking; use HTTPS sites or a VPN; turn off sharing

Public computers

  • Don't let the browser save your password, and uncheck "remember me".
  • Use a private window (InPrivate or Incognito) if you can.
  • Always sign out of every account and close the browser when you finish.
  • Clear the history, cookies, temporary files, and form data if you used a normal window.
  • Don't save files on the public computer, and watch for people looking over your shoulder.

Social engineering and phishing

  • Social engineering tricks people, not computers: a caller pretending to be tech support, a fake boss email, or someone who wants to "verify" your account.
  • Phishing is a fake email, text, or website that looks real so you'll type your password or card number. Texts are called smishing; phone calls are vishing.
  • Red flags: urgent threats, "click here to avoid closing your account", misspellings, a sender address that doesn't match, and links to odd web addresses.
  • Protect yourself: don't click; go to the real website yourself; call the company using a number you trust; never give a PIN or password over the phone; report it.

Protection tools

ToolWhat it doesRemember
Antivirus / anti-malwareScans for and removes viruses and other malwareKeep it on and updated; run full scans (Windows Security / Defender is built in)
FirewallFilters network traffic and blocks unwanted connectionsHardware firewalls are in routers; software firewalls are on the PC. Keep it on
Monitoring softwareTracks activity (parents, employers)Can also be used as spyware
HTTPS (padlock)Encrypts the connection to a websiteLook for https:// before entering payment info
VPNCreates an encrypted tunnel to a trusted network over the internetUse on public Wi-Fi or to reach a work network from home
  • A firewall that's set too strict can block apps you need, so a program may ask you to allow it through.
  • Safe online shopping: buy from known stores, be skeptical of deals that seem too good, use a credit card or trusted payment service, and check for HTTPS.

Backup and restore

ToolWhat it savesUse it to
File History (Windows)Copies of your personal files, saved on a schedule to another driveGet back an older version or a deleted file
Backup and Restore (Windows 7)Scheduled backups of files and a system imageRestore files or the whole PC
System Restore pointSystem files, drivers, and settings (not your documents)Undo a bad update, driver, or install

  • Back up often and keep a copy somewhere else (another drive, the cloud). The 3-2-1 idea: 3 copies, 2 kinds of media, 1 off-site.
  • Encrypt or password-protect backups that hold private information, and keep backup drives in a safe place.

Factory reset

  • A factory reset returns a device to how it was when new: your files, apps, and settings are erased.
  • Windows 10/11: Settings > Update & Security > Recovery > Reset this PC (Windows 11: System > Recovery). You can keep your files or remove everything.
  • Phones: Settings > System > Reset (Android) or Settings > General > Transfer or Reset iPhone.
  • Back up first. Reset before you sell or give away a device, or when nothing else fixes a serious problem.

Troubleshooting basics

ProblemTry first

Everything acting strangeRestart the computer
Problem began after a changeUndo it, uninstall the new program, or use System Restore

  • When you ask for help, describe it clearly: what you were doing, the exact error message, when it started, and what you already tried.
  • Windows has built-in troubleshooters (Settings > Troubleshoot) for internet, sound, printers, and more.

✅ Know for the test

  • Strong passwords are long and mixed; never reuse or share them.
  • Virus needs a file; worm spreads itself; Trojan pretends to be good; spyware watches; ransomware demands payment.
  • Firewall blocks traffic; antivirus removes malware. Keep both on and updated.
  • HTTPS and the padlock mean the connection is encrypted. Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi.
  • System Restore fixes system settings but doesn't back up your files.
  • Factory reset erases everything; back up first and do it before selling a device.
  • Troubleshoot simple to complex: check power and cables, then restart.

Answer: Ransomware.

Q2. Which spreads by itself across a network without you opening anything: a virus or a worm?

Answer: A worm.

Q3. You are on airport Wi-Fi and need to check your bank. What should you use?

Answer: A VPN (or wait for a trusted network); at least make sure the site uses HTTPS.
